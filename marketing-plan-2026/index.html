The strategy section · Verne Harnish, Scaling Up

A brand promise is only a promise if something is at risk

Harnish's second and third strata of strategy: the one promise you make, and the guarantee that proves you mean it. Domino's did not say "fast pizza". It said thirty minutes or it is free.

Where we are

"Fixed fast"

Accurate, memorable and completely unenforceable. No threshold, no clock, no remedy. If we fail it, nothing happens.

What it costs us

No proof, no memory

A promise with no guarantee cannot be verified, so it cannot be repeated, referred or defended. It behaves like decoration.

What we could guarantee

One, measurable

On time or a defined credit. Same-day attendance or the call-out is free. Fixed price quoted before work starts. Membership honoured without argument.

  • The test for a real guarantee: cheap when we are good, expensive only when we are wrong, measured without a committee, and promised in public.The decision we need from Paul is which single promise we are willing to be held to.

Verne Harnish, Scaling Up, 7 Strata of Strategy: brand promise and brand promise guarantee, carried into the One Page Strategic Plan.

How brands grow · Byron Sharp
We rent demand. We do not create it.
Mental availability

We exist only at the moment of search

Growth comes from being retrieved in more buying situations, by more people, including the ones not shopping today. We are filed under "plumber near me", so we are only thought of once the problem is already urgent.

Physical availability

Easy to find, easy to buy

Five sites, five phone numbers, one office hours window. No after-hours booking, no single front door, no reason to pick us over the next result when the customer is ready.

  • Most growth comes from light, occasional buyers, not from our loyal few.Which means reach and memory matter more than another conversion tweak on the same keyword.

Byron Sharp, How Brands Grow. Ehrenberg-Bass Institute: growth is driven by penetration and mental availability, not by deepening loyalty among existing customers.

Always-on versus campaign

A system that runs, not spikes that stop

Campaign

Borrowed attention

Spend goes up, enquiries follow, spend stops, silence. Cost per acquisition rises at every restart because the memory has decayed and the audience has to be bought again. Capacity planning is impossible.

Always-on

Compounding memory

A steady calendar of seasonal service, reminders, reviews and local content that keeps us present in the buying situations we currently miss. Predictable flow, falling acquisition cost over time.

  • What always-on looks like here: pre-summer air conditioning service, twelve-year hot water replacement reminders, autumn roof inspections, membership nurture, review generation after every job, suburb-level content, and a light brand presence all year.
  • Campaigns sit on top of the system.Peak season and offers get a push. They never replace the base.

Top and bottom of funnel

We pay for the five percent who are already looking

Bottom of funnel

Harvesting demand

Google search captures the small share of the market with an active problem. It converts well because the intent is already there, and it gets more expensive every year as more trades bid on the same words. It builds no memory and no defence.

Top of funnel

Creating demand

Broad reach, local brand presence and useful content build the memory that decides who gets searched for next time. It is slower, cheaper per person reached, and it is the only way to stop competing on keyword price forever.

  • Starting ratio: 70% activation, 30% brand for twelve months.The category benchmark is closer to 60:40, and we start from zero brand investment, so the first year leans to brand deliberately. Then measure and adjust.
  • Every dollar at the bottom buys a customer someone else created.Every dollar at the top creates one for us to harvest later.

Benchmark: Binet and Field, The Long and the Short of It, roughly 60% brand to 40% activation for sustained growth. Our current split is effectively 100 to zero.

Appendix

Sources, windows and the arithmetic

Gross profit definitions in use

Four definitions coexist and disagree by more than thirty points. This deck ranks on job-level GP, Paul's method: revenue ex GST less labour at actual working and driving hours at each technician's burden rate, less materials, subbies and recalls, on completed jobs. The monthly Xero view runs at 37 to 43 percent because it carries advertising, fleet, fuel and card fees below the gross profit line. The performance dashboard's growth matrix is a third, book-based view at roughly 37 percent of revenue. None of them is wrong; they answer different questions. The ranking is stable under all four.

Data windows

Revenue: invoiced, ServiceTitan report 295, trailing 12 months to October 2026. Job-level GP: completed jobs, trailing 12 months, allocation refreshed 14 October 2026. Member economics: live window November 2024 to October 2026, 1,316 active members against 8,714 non-members. Paid channel split: 90 days to 27 October 2026. Conversion funnel: 90 days to 27 October 2026.

Known data gaps

Materials allocation is not fully audited, so job-level GP is sound on labour and marginally generous on materials. ServiceTitan job history begins at the November 2024 migration, so repeat and loyalty figures are a floor, not a full history. Meta has no daily spend feed wired for the service brands, so "zero" means zero in the tracked accounts, not proof of no activity elsewhere. The competitor study is dated December 2024.

Market size arithmetic

Dwellings: 593,881 private dwellings in Greater Adelaide at the 2021 census, grown 1.2 percent a year to approximately 630,000 in 2026.

Air conditioning: 85 percent penetration, 536,000 homes, replacement cycle 15 years, 35,700 replacements a year at $6,700 = $239m. Service at $230 every two years = $62m. Total $301m.

Plumbing and hot water: 75 percent of homes with a storage or heat pump system, replaced every 12 years, 39,400 units at $2,200 = $87m. Repairs: 35 percent of homes call a plumber each year at $450 = $99m. Total $186m.

Roofing: 70 percent of homes with a replaceable roof, 30 year cycle, 14,700 roofs at $18,000 = $265m.

Electrical and solar: 50 percent of SA houses with solar, 15 year replacement cycle, 21,000 systems at $9,000 = $189m.

Renovations: $677m of permit-approved SA renovations, 75 percent in Greater Adelaide, 30 percent bathroom and kitchen = $152m.

Each line is an order-of-magnitude estimate built to show scale and relative share, not a forecast. The penetration rates and cycles are the assumptions to challenge first.
