How this was built

03Video

What role video plays

This is the question worth answering properly, because the instinct is that video wins and the data says something more specific than that.

59%of genuine recruitment ads are video
88%of recruitment video is 9:16 vertical
27smedian video length
32 vs 113median days running, video vs static

What the numbers say

Video is roughly one in five recruitment ads, not the default.

Of everything the keywords surface about a third is video, but that includes trailers, lighting and product ads. Once you filter to genuine recruitment, 16 of 27 are video. Video is common and growing, but static is still carrying the category.

The format is settled: vertical, 9:16, and nothing else.

Fourteen of sixteen recruitment videos are 360x640 vertical. One is landscape and one is 4:5. Landscape is effectively dead here, and the single landscape ad in the set is the long testimonial that would have performed better cropped.

Recruitment video is short: median 27 seconds.

Three quarters sit under 30 seconds and a quarter under 15. Compare that to the wider video pool where the median is 69 seconds and 39% run past three minutes, because those are product ads. Nobody is asking a sparky to watch a five minute film to find out about a job.

Video does not outlast static. This is the counterintuitive one.

Median days running is 32 for video against 113 for static. Sixty two per cent of videos have run past 30 days against seventy per cent of statics. So video is not the durability play. Its job is reach and attention, getting the role in front of people cheaply, while static carries the long haul and the detail.

Every single video uses real people. Nobody is generating them.

Existing staff and owners, to camera, in branded polos and high-vis, cut with footage of real crews on real jobs, every time. Not one generated human in the entire set. In a trade whose whole pitch is a real team and a real training wall, that is the right instinct and it should shape our tooling decisions.

The production bar is very low, which is the opportunity.

Most are a phone held to a face in a ute. Exactly one advertiser bought a drone shot and they have been running 43 days on it. A properly shot vertical with real crew footage would stand out immediately in this market.

    What the codification actually tells us

    The take-home vehicle appears in 58% of the ads running 60 days or more. It is the single most common offer in the market, more common than pay itself.

    The frustration-mirror hook, the one that names the candidate's current problem rather than announcing a vacancy, is 33% of the long-running set against only 19% of the whole library. It over-performs its share, which makes it the highest value angle to test.

    A plain hiring announcement is the default at 59% of everything published, but it falls to 58% of the survivors, so it is not the differentiator it feels like.

    05Patterns

    The patterns that repeat

    What the long-running ads do in common. Not one of them is a clever visual trick.

    Pay is stated outright

    Five of the eleven statics name a number. $38 to $45 an hour, $110k to $135k a year, up to $65 an hour in Queensland. The ones that hide the rate are the ones that do not last.

    Perks are itemised, never summarised

    The long-running ads list eight to ten single-line perks with ticks. Nobody writes generous package. They write the take-home ute, the fuel card, the birthday off, the Thursday finish.

    The take-home vehicle is the top currency

    It is the perk that gets named first and described in most detail. Bice leads with the van and the tool kit; Activ8 leads with personal use on the fuel card.

    Time beats money as a differentiator

    Five of eleven sell the schedule: four-day weeks, TOIL banking, RDOs, early Friday finishes, Monday to Thursday. At this pay level time is the thing competitors cannot easily match.

    The hook is your current employer, not us

    The strongest copy names the reader's private frustration. Does your boss actually value your work. Ready to enjoy being a sparky again. It reads like a person, not a job spec.

    Location is named by suburb, not by state

    Unley and the coastline south of the Adelaide CBD. Moreton Bay. Mount Gambier. Coffs Harbour. Specific geography signals a real local job to someone scrolling in that area.

    Non-negotiables are framed as integrity

    We do follow the rules. Certificate III, sorry, non-negotiable. Stating the hard requirements plainly reads as a company that will not cut corners, which tradespeople screen for.

    The winning creative is real people

    Uniforms, branded vans, real crews on real jobs, in both the statics and every video. Nobody in this sweep is running generated people, and in a trade selling a real team that is the right call.

    06The gaps

    Where aba TRADES can win

    The gaps in the set. Every one of them is something we already hold the raw material for.

    Nobody makes the vehicle the hero image

    Bice names the van in copy but the creative is a group photo holding signs. A clean shot of a kitted-out take-home ute with the perk line on it is an unclaimed visual.

    Almost nobody shows real team photography well

    Only two advertisers use real people, and neither puts genuine craft into it. Our SharePoint library holds 65 professional electrical on-site shots at up to 8192px.

    • The full creative and the full primary text
    • The advertiser's page, and its follower count

    How the instant form works, and why it is so cheap

    The whole application happens inside Facebook or Instagram. There is no page load and nothing to leave the app for. The fields are prefilled with what Meta already knows, and the detail a candidate needs comes from the ad copy itself plus a short intro line on the form. Fewer steps, nothing to load, nothing to lose. What it gives up is depth, because a landing page can carry the full offer, the crew film and the wall, and the form cannot.

    So the trade is clean: the instant form should win on volume and cost, the landing page should win on intent and quality. That is a testable claim, and every ad in this plan tests it rather than assuming it either way.

    Every ad runs twice, as a controlled comparison

    Same creative, same copy, same audience, separate ad sets: one variant with a web form CTA, one with a landing page CTA. Change one thing at a time or the result is unreadable. Every card in the electrical set names its two variants.

    Judge it on cost per qualified applicant, not cost per lead

    The instant form will very probably produce the cheaper lead. The question is whether it produces the cheaper interview. Track through to the application and the interview, which ServiceTitan can carry, and let that decide. On cost per lead alone this comparison has already been won once and tells us nothing new.

    If we test a page, the page has to earn its place

    The offer, the wall in use, the crew film, and real answers on pay and the roster. A thin page loses to the form every time and proves nothing about the destination.

    The destination is the first decision, not the last

    Instant form, optimised for leads, is the default for every concept. A landing page gets tested once against it, not assumed to be better.

    Nothing goes out optimised for clicks, reach or engagement

    The account has already paid $593 to learn that lesson. A recruitment objective, every time.

    The crew film moves up, but not alone

    Real people talking is proven on our own account, and a real person on a static is proven cheapest. Both get built; neither replaces the other.

    No concept is retired on cost per lead alone

    A good asset on a website looks like a bad asset. The Team Interviews video is the proof: excellent attention, lost at the door. Judge the asset only once the destination is right.

    What this cannot tell us

    Being straight about the limits, because these are the things worth chasing next.

    • Nothing joins to a hire. These are leads, not people who started. ServiceTitan holds the other end, and joining the two is the analysis worth doing.
    • Mixed result types. Reach, link clicks, messaging conversations, website contacts and instant form leads are all being called a result by the platform. They are not comparable, which is why a blended cost per result across everything is meaningless.
    • The sample is small and the spend is modest. Twenty four ads and about eleven thousand dollars over three years. The direction is unmistakable, the precision is not.
    • No daily breakdown in this view. These are lifetime totals per ad, so we cannot read fatigue, seasonality or what was live alongside what.
    • Attribution differs slightly between ads. Most are seven day click or one day view, a few add one day engaged view. Close enough to compare, not identical.

    11Mini game

    The mini game: a playable ad funnel

    The board is built and playable. Open it on a phone: harley-abatrades.github.io/abatrades-pages/challenge/electrical/. It is running as a review build, not live, and it carries its own review flag.

    The ad itself is E6 in the electrical set above. This section is the reasoning behind it: why the format is worth owning, how the funnel runs, what it takes to build and how we judge it.

    A short playable switchboard challenge on its own microsite. The ad sells curiosity rather than the job. The board is built from the offer itself: each of the four circuits is one thing the job gives you, so the player assembles the offer by playing instead of being shouted at.

    Why it should work

    It is the only unoccupied format in the market

    Every one of the 27 ads we profiled is a picture, a video or a block of text. Nothing interactive exists anywhere in the sweep. In a category where everyone is writing the same perk list, being the only advertiser with something to do is worth more than any headline.

    The game qualifies the audience for us

    Somebody who works a switchboard is an electrician, and the board tells us who actually plays. That is a self selecting filter doing part of the work an application form cannot do.

    It gives people a reason to want the offer

    Instead of shouting a rate at a stranger, we let them prove something first and then make the offer. It lands on someone already engaged, and already flattered.

    Trades are competitive and proud

    A challenge is a natural invitation to this audience in a way a job ad never is. It also gives us a shareable link, so the trade circulates it for us.

    It measures better than a static ad

    Completion rate, round drop off and time on page tell us who is genuinely interested. A static ad tells us nothing past the click.

    The game itself

    Three rounds, under a minute, one thumb. Round one is not there to test anybody, it is there to make a qualified electrician feel like one. Round three is the one that gets screenshotted.

    The board

    A switchboard with four tripped RCBOs and the main switch already on. Flick each one up. Nothing blocks the first move and there is no trick, which is deliberate: it flatters a qualified sparky rather than testing him.

    The circuits

    Every circuit is part of the offer, not a puzzle. C1 PAY, C2 UTE, C3 TRAINING, C4 HOME. Flick one and that benefit lights up with its own line underneath it.

    The finish

    The board reads back "C4 on and holding. All four on." Four circuits live, and the applicant has just built the offer himself.

    The funnel, step by step

    1. The ad

    Vertical screen recording of the board being played, fast, the RCBOs flicking up. Short copy and no offer in it. The only promise is that there is a board worth getting going.

    2. The microsite

    Loads straight onto the board. No form, no email, no gate before playing. One screen, works with one thumb, no loading state.

    3. The reveal

    As each circuit goes on its line appears: the flat rate, the ute and card, the 25 metre training wall, work close to home. The offer arrives as the reward for playing.

    4. The offer

    The four lines are the offer: $60 an hour flat with T&Cs, a company ute and a fuel card, the 25 metre in-house training wall since 2015, and work close to home.

    5. The ask

    Apply sits on the board the whole time, with a skip that reads "Keen already? No need to play.", so nobody is ever forced through the game. The application itself is the five fields on the careers page.

    6. The share

    A "Challenge a mate" button. It costs us nothing and it circulates inside the trade, which is where electricians actually talk to each other.

    What it takes to build

    What it is

    One page. SVG and plain JavaScript, no game engine, no framework and no third party script, so it runs on our own host and can move to the client site later.

    Where it stands

    Built and playable, running as a review build with its own review flag rather than live. harley-abatrades.github.io/abatrades-pages/challenge/electrical/

    What it still needs

    The screen recording for the ad cut, and the Apply button pointed at the live application form. Nothing else on the build is outstanding.

    Effort

    The build is done. What is left is the ad cut, and that is a screen recording rather than a shoot.

    Risks, and how we handle them

    More friction than a form

    A game is a bigger ask than a click to a form, so click through may look worse on paper. Judge it on applications and on cost per qualified applicant. Never on click rate.

    It has to be good, or it is worse than nothing

    A clunky quiz damages the brand more than a plain ad ever could. One thumb, one screen, no loading, no dead ends, and genuinely enjoyable within the first three seconds.

    Do not gate the offer behind the game

    The build already carries the skip, reading "Keen already? No need to play." next to the Apply button, so nobody is ever forced through it. Keep it that way.

    Mobile first, or it fails

    It will be played on a phone, one handed, probably on site. Test at 390px before anything else.

    No data collection before the end

    Nothing tracked, no email harvesting, no cookie wall. Play for free, share details only when they choose to apply.

    How we judge it

    Ad click through, board start rate, circuits flicked, apply clicks, and cost per applicant against the straight offer ads. The build already tracks each flick separately (flick_1 to flick_4), so drop off per circuit is readable and we can see which benefit people stop at. If flicking is high and applications are low, the offer is the problem, not the game.

    12Plan

    How we would run them

    One always-on campaign for the offer ads, one for the challenge, and one for tone. The challenge runs against the same target but through a different door, so the two can be compared on cost per qualified applicant.

    Campaign 1 · The offer

    E1 to E4 together in one always-on ad set. Let the platform allocate. Four creatives is enough to learn which angle carries without starving any of them. Each runs twice, web form CTA and landing page CTA, in separate ad sets.

    Campaign 2 · The mini game

    E6, pointed at its own microsite rather than the application page. Separate budget, so we can read its cost per applicant against campaign 1 honestly.

    Campaign 3 · Culture

    E5 as a low cost always-on presence. It carries both electrical video cuts, and both run. The third vertical now sits with the plumbing set as P4, where its branding belongs.

    Media

    Three video ads in total: E5A and E5B under the electrical set, and P4 under plumbing. Everything else is a static at 1080x1350 and 1080x1080. We are not making a video version of each ad: static already carries the offer at the costs above, and the video cuts do the reach and attention job the research says video is for.

    Destination and objective

    Every concept runs its web form variant on an instant form optimised for leads, and never for clicks, reach or engagement. The landing page variant runs beside it as the controlled comparison, so the destination question is answered by our own numbers rather than by preference. On the account so far the instant form has won that comparison by about twenty times, which is why it stays the default until a landing page beats it.

    The discipline bit

    Judge on applications, never on how good the creative looks. The Ad Library tells us what competitors run and nothing about how it performed, so our own numbers have to come from the ad account.

    What I would run first

    E2 and E3. E2 is the flat rate the whole market is too nervous to state, and E3 is the one claim nobody can answer. E1 goes up alongside them.

    Then build the challenge. It is the only idea on this page that a competitor cannot copy inside a week, and it is the one that brings us candidates nobody else is reaching.
